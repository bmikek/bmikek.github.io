Dataset: Flight Delay and Cancellation Dataset (2019–2023) — Kaggle / U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics

1. Introduction

Flight delays impose significant costs on passengers and airlines alike. This project applies supervised machine learning techniques to the Flight Delay and Cancellation Dataset (2019–2023), a 3-million-record sample sourced from the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics. The central research question is: given information available at the time of booking — airline, route, scheduled departure time, and historical delay patterns — how accurately can we predict a flight’s arrival delay in minutes?

The dataset contains 32 columns covering flight identifiers, scheduled and actual times, delay breakdowns by cause (carrier, weather, NAS, security, late aircraft), and cancellation codes. It spans five years of domestic U.S. air travel, bridging pre-pandemic (2019), pandemic (2020–2021), and recovery (2022–2023) periods.

2. Exploratory Data Analysis

2.1 Target Variable: Arrival Delay

The target variable, ARR_DELAY, measures the difference between actual and scheduled arrival time in minutes, where negative values indicate early arrivals. Summary statistics on the 2.91 million non-missing records revealed a highly skewed distribution:

•        Mean: 4.26 minutes

•        Median: −7.0 minutes (the typical flight arrives early)

•        Standard deviation: 51.2 minutes

•        Range: −96 to 2,934 minutes

 

Plotting the full distribution confirmed a strong right skew with a long tail of extreme delays. The bulk of flights clustered just left of zero, reflecting that most flights arrive on time or slightly early. Examining breakpoints further:

•        66.4% of flights arrived on time or early (ARR_DELAY ≤ 0)

•        17.7% were delayed by more than 15 minutes

•        6.0% were delayed by more than 60 minutes

 

This distribution has important modeling implications: the majority of variance is driven by a minority of badly delayed flights, and any model must contend with this skew.

2.2 Missingness

Missingness analysis identified several structural patterns. The delay-cause breakdown columns (DELAY_DUE_CARRIER, DELAY_DUE_WEATHER, etc.) were missing for 82.2% of records — these fields are only populated for delayed flights and were excluded from modeling to avoid data leakage. ARR_DELAY itself was missing for 2.9% of records (86,198 rows), the majority of which corresponded to the 79,140 cancelled flights in the dataset. An additional 7,058 records had null ARR_DELAY despite not being flagged as cancelled, likely diversions. All missing-target rows were removed before modeling.

2.3 Delay by Airline

Mean arrival delay varied substantially across carriers. Allegiant Air showed the highest mean delay at 13.3 minutes, followed by JetBlue (12.3 min) and Frontier (11.1 min). At the other end of the spectrum, Endeavor Air was the only carrier with a negative mean delay (−1.3 min), indicating it typically arrives early. Delta, Republic Airline, and Alaska Airlines also showed very low mean delays (all under 1.1 minutes).

Notably, the median delay was negative for every single airline, meaning more than half of all flights for every carrier arrive early. This suggests that the mean is being pulled upward by a relatively small number of severe delay events rather than a broad pattern of lateness.

2.4 Delay by Month

Monthly delay patterns were consistent with expectations. Summer months (June–August) and winter months (December–February) showed elevated mean delays, driven by weather-related disruptions and holiday-season congestion respectively. Spring months (March–May) and early fall (September–October) were the most punctual periods, consistent with known patterns in U.S. aviation.

2.5 Delay by Departure Hour

A clear accumulation effect was visible across the day: early morning flights (departing before 8 AM) had the lowest mean delays, while flights departing in the late afternoon and evening (3–9 PM) had significantly higher mean delays. This reflects the well-known ripple effect in aviation, where delays cascade through the day as aircraft rotate through multiple flights.

2.6 Early Arrivals

An analysis of early arrivals revealed that 64.6% of flights arrived early, with an average early arrival of 14.0 minutes. Only 1.8% of flights arrived exactly on schedule, and 33.6% arrived late, averaging 36.5 minutes late. This asymmetry — where early arrivals are more common but late arrivals are more extreme — reflects deliberate schedule padding by airlines.

Endeavor Air had the highest early-arrival rate at 75.1%, followed by Republic Airline (69.7%) and Delta (69.4%). Allegiant Air and Hawaiian Airlines had the lowest early-arrival rates, consistent with their higher mean delay figures. Strikingly, even JetBlue, which had the second-highest mean delay, still had 57.9% of its flights arrive early, underlining how severely a subset of extreme delays pulls the mean upward for some carriers.

3. Feature Transformation

3.1 Outlier Capping

ARR_DELAY was capped at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles (−41 to 258 minutes) to reduce the influence of extreme outliers — such as the recorded maximum of 2,934 minutes — which would otherwise dominate squared-error loss functions.

3.2 Cyclical Encoding of Time Features

Month of year and departure hour are inherently cyclical: December is closer to January than it is to June, and 11 PM is closer to midnight than to noon. Encoding these as raw integers would impose a false linear structure. Both features were instead encoded as sine/cosine pairs:

•        MONTH_SIN / MONTH_COS: sin(2π × month / 12) and cos(2π × month / 12)

•        HOUR_SIN / HOUR_COS: sin(2π × hour / 24) and cos(2π × hour / 24)

 

This allows models to correctly capture the wrap-around nature of time without treating January (1) and December (12) as maximally different.

3.3 Target Encoding for Categorical Variables

Airline and origin airport are high-cardinality categorical variables. Rather than one-hot encoding (which would produce hundreds of sparse columns), each was replaced with its historical mean ARR_DELAY from the dataset. This condenses each category into a single informative numeric signal:

•        AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN: mean arrival delay for each carrier

•        ORIGIN_DELAY_MEAN: mean arrival delay for each origin airport

 

These features carry the most predictive information from the categorical variables while keeping the feature space compact. In a production setting, these encoding values should be computed on training folds only to prevent target leakage.

3.4 Log Transformation of Distance

Flight distance (in miles) was log-transformed to DISTANCE_LOG using log(1 + distance), reducing right skew and making the relationship between distance and delay more linear. The log scale better captures the diminishing return on delay risk as flights get longer — the difference between a 200-mile and 400-mile flight matters more than the difference between a 1,800-mile and 2,000-mile flight.

3.5 Interaction Term

An interaction term AIRLINE_x_HOUR was constructed as the product of AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN and HOUR_SIN. This captures the hypothesis that certain airlines may be disproportionately affected by time-of-day congestion effects — for example, a typically late carrier may suffer even more in peak hours. This term became one of the most important features in the final model.

3.6 Standardization

All features were standardized (zero mean, unit variance) using StandardScaler before being passed to linear and regularized models. Tree-based models (Decision Tree and Random Forest) do not require standardization and were fit on raw feature values.

4. Feature Selection

4.1 Initial Feature Set

The final candidate feature set was:

•        MONTH_SIN, MONTH_COS (cyclical month)

•        HOUR_SIN, HOUR_COS (cyclical departure hour)

•        DAY_OF_WEEK

•        AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN (target-encoded carrier)

•        ORIGIN_DELAY_MEAN (target-encoded origin airport)

•        DISTANCE_LOG (log flight distance)

•        AIRLINE_x_HOUR (interaction: carrier × time-of-day)

 

Delay-cause breakdown columns (DELAY_DUE_CARRIER, DELAY_DUE_WEATHER, etc.) were deliberately excluded: these are only populated after a delay occurs, meaning including them would constitute data leakage — the model would be predicting delay using information only available after the fact.

4.2 Correlation Analysis

A correlation matrix showed no severe multicollinearity among the retained features. The highest correlation with ARR_DELAY was for AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN and ORIGIN_DELAY_MEAN, consistent with expectations. The cyclical time features showed modest correlations, capturing the time-of-day and seasonal effects seen in EDA.

4.3 LASSO Feature Selection

LASSO regression (alpha = 0.5) on standardized features was used to identify which variables contributed meaningfully to prediction. Three features were eliminated (coefficient set to zero):

•        MONTH_SIN — zeroed out (MONTH_COS captured the seasonal signal)

•        HOUR_COS — zeroed out (HOUR_SIN carried the time-of-day effect)

•        DISTANCE_LOG — zeroed out in the linear model, though it emerged as the top feature in the Random Forest (see Section 6)

 

The six surviving features were: HOUR_SIN, AIRLINE_x_HOUR, MONTH_COS, DAY_OF_WEEK, ORIGIN_DELAY_MEAN, and AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN. The strongest LASSO coefficients were for AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN (+1.88) and ORIGIN_DELAY_MEAN (+1.36), confirming that carrier identity and origin airport are the most important linear predictors of delay.

5.2 Best Model: Random Forest

The Random Forest regressor performed best across all three metrics, achieving an RMSE of 37.76 minutes, MAE of 22.04 minutes, and R² of 0.030. While its margin over the next-best model (Decision Tree) was modest, the Random Forest’s ensemble nature makes it more robust to overfitting and better at capturing non-linear interactions between features.

Feature importances from the Random Forest (ranked highest to lowest):

•        DISTANCE_LOG (25.7%): the strongest single predictor, suggesting flight distance captures route-level structural delay risk not fully encoded by the airline/airport means

•        AIRLINE_x_HOUR (18.8%): the interaction between carrier and time-of-day was the second most important feature, validating the decision to include it

•        ORIGIN_DELAY_MEAN (16.5%): origin airport delay history was a strong predictor

•        DAY_OF_WEEK (10.6%): day of the week contributed meaningfully

•        MONTH_COS (7.7%), MONTH_SIN (5.7%): seasonal effects were moderate

•        HOUR_COS (5.3%), HOUR_SIN (5.0%): time-of-day effects were captured

•        AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN (4.7%): carrier-level mean delay was the weakest feature in the tree model, likely because it is partially subsumed by AIRLINE_x_HOUR

 

5.3 Linear Model Interpretation

Standardized linear regression coefficients confirmed the importance of AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN and ORIGIN_DELAY_MEAN as the strongest positive predictors: higher historical delay for a carrier or airport strongly predicts a later arrival. HOUR_SIN and the AIRLINE_x_HOUR interaction term had negative standardized coefficients, reflecting the non-linear relationship between time of day and delay in a linear framework. These signs should be interpreted with caution given the low overall R² of the linear model.

5.4 Classification Extension

A binary classification task was also explored: predicting whether a flight would arrive more than 15 minutes late (IS_LATE). At a 15-minute threshold, 17.7% of flights were classified as late. This framing is practically useful — a traveler may care more about “will I miss my connection?” than the precise number of minutes. Classification models and metrics (F1, ROC-AUC, Precision, Recall) were computed, providing a complementary perspective to the regression analysis.

6. Hypothesis Evaluation

The original hypothesis stated: “Scheduled departure time and airline carrier will be the strongest predictors of arrival delay magnitude.”

The results partially contradict this hypothesis. Departure time (via HOUR_SIN/HOUR_COS) and airline (via AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN) are indeed important features, but neither emerged as the dominant predictor in the Random Forest. Instead, DISTANCE_LOG (25.7%) ranked first, and the AIRLINE_x_HOUR interaction term (18.8%) ranked second. ORIGIN_DELAY_MEAN (16.5%) and DAY_OF_WEEK (10.6%) also outranked standalone airline and time-of-day features.

This suggests that route-level factors — where a flight goes and from which airport it departs — are at least as important as carrier identity in predicting delay. The finding is consistent with real-world aviation dynamics: congested hub airports and long-haul routes face structural delay risks that persist regardless of carrier.

7. Shortcomings & Future Research

7.1 Low Explanatory Power

The most significant limitation of this analysis is the low R² across all models (maximum 0.030). This means the models explain only about 3% of the variance in arrival delay. This is not surprising given the features available: the most powerful real-world predictors of delay — real-time weather conditions, air traffic control decisions, and mechanical issues — are not knowable at booking time and were not included. The models are fundamentally constrained by what a traveler can know in advance.

7.2 Target Encoding Leakage Risk

The target-encoded airline and airport features were computed on the full dataset rather than on training folds only. In a rigorous pipeline, these means should be computed separately for each fold to avoid information leakage from the test set into training.

7.3 Pandemic Anomaly

The 2020–2021 period saw dramatically reduced flight volumes and atypical delay patterns due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Including these years may distort historical mean features (like AIRLINE_DELAY_MEAN), potentially affecting model generalizability to normal operating conditions.

7.4 Interesting Follow-Up Research

•        Incorporate NOAA weather data: matching each flight to hourly weather observations at its origin airport at departure time would likely be the single largest improvement to predictive power.

•        Separate pre- vs. post-pandemic models: training on 2019-only vs. 2022–2023 data could reveal whether airline reliability patterns fundamentally shifted after the pandemic, as staffing shortages may have restructured which carriers and routes are most prone to delay.

•        Route-level features: incorporating origin-destination pair-level historical delay means, rather than just origin airport means, could capture route-specific congestion patterns more precisely.

•        Predict delay cause category: a multi-class classification model predicting whether a delay is due to carrier, weather, NAS, or late aircraft could be useful for actionable recommendations to both travelers and airlines.

 

8. Conclusion

This project applied five regression models to predict U.S. domestic flight arrival delay using pre-departure booking information. Through EDA, we found that the typical flight arrives early, that extreme delays are concentrated among a minority of flights, and that carrier identity, departure hour, and origin airport all show meaningful associations with delay. Feature engineering — including cyclical time encoding, target encoding, log-distance transformation, and an airline×hour interaction term — produced a compact, informative feature set.

The best-performing model was a Random Forest regressor (RMSE = 37.76 min, R² = 0.030). While the low R² reflects the fundamental unpredictability of flight delays from pre-departure information alone, the relative feature importances provide meaningful insights: route distance, carrier–time interactions, and airport-level delay history are the strongest available predictors. The hypothesis that airline and departure time would dominate was partially contradicted — distance and origin airport effects proved equally or more important.

Future work incorporating real-time weather data holds the greatest promise for meaningfully improving predictive accuracy.
